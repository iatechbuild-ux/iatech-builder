# Research Basis for Curriculum v3

## Purpose

This note records the external standards used to test and strengthen the IATECH curriculum. The standards inform the design; they do not replace IATECH's local, problem-first identity.

## Sources and adopted implications

### UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (2024)

UNESCO organizes student AI competence across a human-centred mindset, ethics, techniques and applications, and system design, progressing through Understand, Apply, and Create.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- AI use must include human agency, accountability, privacy, bias, and verification.
- Prompting alone is not AI competence.
- Learners progress from understanding AI limits, to responsible application, to designing AI-assisted solutions.
- AI evidence must show decisions made by the learner, not only the generated output.

Source: https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-competency-framework-students

### European Commission DigComp

DigComp defines digital competence as confident, critical, and responsible technology use. Its five areas cover information and data literacy, communication and collaboration, content creation, safety, and problem solving.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- Safe search, source evaluation, file management, privacy, and digital identity are prerequisites, not optional extras.
- Every pathway must include safety and information-literacy outcomes.
- Tool operation alone is insufficient; learners must judge relevance, reliability, and consequences.

Source: https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/oldpage-digcomp/digcomp-framework_en

### CSTA K-12 Computer Science Standards

CSTA covers computing systems, networks and the internet, data and analysis, algorithms and programming, and impacts of computing. Its practices emphasize defining computational problems, developing abstractions, creating artifacts, testing, communicating, and inclusive collaboration.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- Web and Python courses must teach decomposition, data, control structures, modularity, testing, and impacts—not isolated syntax.
- Learners must compare choices and explain trade-offs.
- Accessibility, privacy, licensing, and societal impact belong inside technical work.

Source: https://csteachers.org/k12standards/

### W3C Web Accessibility Initiative Curricula

W3C's developer curriculum covers semantic structure, navigation, images, tables, forms, widgets, keyboard interaction, focus, and notifications.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- Accessibility is a core web-development outcome and a release gate.
- Every shipped website must pass keyboard, structure, label, contrast, and responsive checks.
- Accessibility must be assessed in the artifact, not only recalled in a quiz.

Source: https://www.w3.org/WAI/curricula/developer-modules/

### CAST Universal Design for Learning Guidelines 3.0

CAST emphasizes multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression, with the goal of learner agency.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- A learner may explain through text, audio, annotated screenshots, or a live tutor conversation when the competency does not require one specific medium.
- Supports should be graduated and removable, not permanent shortcuts.
- Missions must anticipate low bandwidth, disability, language, and device barriers.

Source: https://udlguidelines.cast.org/

### OECD Learning Compass 2030

The Learning Compass emphasizes student agency and the integration of knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values in unfamiliar contexts.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- Independence requires transfer to an unfamiliar problem.
- Learners must make and defend meaningful choices.
- Responsibility and community impact are assessed alongside technical output.

Source: https://www.oecd.org/en/data/tools/oecd-learning-compass-2030.html

### PBLWorks Gold Standard Project-Based Learning

The model identifies a challenging problem, sustained inquiry, authenticity, learner voice and choice, reflection, critique and revision, and a public product.

IATECH adopts these implications:

- Every course includes authentic missions and at least one audience-facing capstone.
- Feedback must lead to a recorded revision.
- Learners receive constrained choice over context, audience, data, or implementation.

Source: https://www.pblworks.org/what-is-pbl/gold-standard-project-design

## Synthesis: the IATECH proof standard

An IATECH course is outcome-ready only when a learner can:

1. diagnose a real problem;
2. select and justify an appropriate tool;
3. build or produce a working artifact;
4. test it against requirements, safety, accessibility, and failure cases;
5. critique and revise it using feedback;
6. disclose and verify AI assistance;
7. transfer the skill to a different context with reduced support; and
8. explain the result to another person.

Completion, attendance, watched content, or a single guided artifact cannot establish this standard.
